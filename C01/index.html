Māori — Tangata Whenua, People of the Land

Māori are the indigenous people of New Zealand, known as tangata whenua — the people of the land. Their deep connection to this country is central to New Zealand's identity, history, and culture. 12

Origins and Early Settlement

The ancestors of Māori arrived by waka (canoe) from Pacific islands before 1300 CE, settling first along the coast where they hunted seals and moa, grew food, and gradually moved into the forests. 3 They lived in small tribal groups with a rich oral culture, strong traditions of warfare, and profound reverence for their ancestors and the gods of the natural world. 34

Māori tribes also carry a wealth of narratives about people and beings who existed long before the arrival of Polynesian settlers — collective knowledge that remains central to the experience and identity of tangata whenua today. 5

The bond between Māori and the land is captured in the expression "Ko au, ko te whenua, ko te whenua, ko au" — "I am the land and the land is me." 6

Traditional Society

Pre-European Māori society was oral and organised around three core units: whānau (family), hapū (sub-tribe), and iwi (tribe). 78 Society was also stratified into social rankings — rangatira (leaders), tūtūā (commoners), and taurekareka or mōkai (slaves) — with rank and leadership based on seniority of descent from founding ancestors. 9

Whakapapa (genealogy) was, and remains, central to Māori tradition. When Māori introduce themselves, they often reference the mountains, lakes, or rivers important to their tribe, grounding their identity firmly in the land. 1011

Kin relationships underpin many social customs. Hapū hold rights over particular marae (communal gathering places), hosting gatherings, weddings, and funerals. Kaumātua and kuia (male and female elders) hold an honoured place in communities. 12

The Arrival of Europeans and the Treaty of Waitangi

The arrival of Europeans from the early 1800s transformed Māori communities significantly. Missionaries arrived, many Māori converted to Christianity, and trade — particularly in pigs and potatoes — expanded rapidly. 3

In 1840, Te Tiriti o Waitangi (the Treaty of Waitangi) was signed between the British Crown and more than 500 Māori chiefs. 13 However, the English and Māori versions of the treaty carried different meanings — the English version gave the Crown sovereignty, while the Māori version guaranteed tino rangatiratanga (chieftainship) over their lands, villages, and treasured possessions. 1314 This difference in understanding eventually led to conflict, land loss, and a long process of reinterpretation that continues today. 15

Following the New Zealand Wars of the 1840s and 1860s, Māori lost vast amounts of land through confiscation and sale. 316

Revival and the 20th Century

In the first half of the 20th century, important leaders such as Apirana Ngata and Te Puea Hērangi worked to improve Māori wellbeing and revive the culture, renewing interest in the language and arts such as carving and weaving. 3

After the Second World War, many Māori moved to cities for work — a major shift from the rural, tribally centred life of previous generations. 8 In the 1970s and 1980s, political activism intensified, with significant events including:

  • A land march down the North Island to Parliament in 1975
  • The creation of the Waitangi Tribunal (1975) to examine alleged breaches of the Treaty
  • The occupation of Bastion Point in Auckland by protesters in 1977–78 3

Treaty Settlements

The Waitangi Tribunal was set up in 1975 to hear claims about Crown breaches of the treaty, and from 1985 could consider historic claims back to 1840. 16 Settlement negotiations led to landmark agreements — including the Sealord fisheries agreement ($170 million) in 1992, and the Waikato Tainui settlement ($170 million) in 1995. Some settlements also included formal Crown apologies and cultural redress such as the restoration of Māori place names — for example, Aoraki/Mt Cook. 16

Māori Today

By 2019, nearly 800,000 Māori people were living in New Zealand, with most in cities. The cultural landscape includes kōhanga reo (preschool language nests), Māori-language schools, two Māori television channels, 21 radio stations, and 29 members of Parliament who identified as Māori. 3

Te reo Māori became an official language of New Zealand in 1987, and a national target of one million te reo speakers by 2040 has been set. 17 The language's revitalisation has been driven by the kōhanga reo generation — young, fluent speakers who, in the words of Māori Language Commission chair Rawinia Higgins, "don't know what it means to not have te reo Māori." 18

From protest to celebration, Māori culture, identity, and language continue to grow and assert their place at the heart of Aotearoa New Zealand. 1819
