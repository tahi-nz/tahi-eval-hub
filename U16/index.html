Ihumātao: Sacred Land, Protest, and a Hard-Won Resolution

Ihumātao — originally known as Te Ihu o Mataoho — is a place of profound historical and archaeological significance on the Manukau Harbour in South Auckland. It sits alongside the Ōtuataua Stonefields Historic Reserve, part of an ancient landscape of Māori kāinga (settlements) and gardens established around the region's volcanic cones. 12

Why the Land Matters

The site carries deep layers of meaning. Auckland once had more than 8,000 hectares of volcanic stonefields like those at Ihumātao, established by Māori as horticultural settlements. Today, fewer than 200 hectares remain — the rest destroyed by 150 years of quarrying and urban development. 3

The land also carries a painful colonial history. During the New Zealand Wars of the 1860s, much of South Auckland and Waikato — including Ihumātao — was confiscated by the Crown from its original owners. The land had been occupied by Waikato Tainui, Ngāti Whātua, and related groups including Ngāti Tamaoho, Te Ahiwaru, Ngāti Tai Tāmaki, Ngāti Te Ata, Te Ākitai Waiohua, and Te Kawerau ā Maki. Local iwi were forced off, and the land passed into private ownership for roughly 150 years. 42

The Protest

In 2014, Auckland Council rezoned 32 hectares of Ihumātao as a Special Housing Area (SHA). The land was subsequently sold to Fletcher Building, which planned to build more than 400 houses on the archaeologically sensitive site. 4

A protest group, Save Our Unique Landscape (SOUL), formed in response and began occupying the site in 2016. Their core arguments were:

  • The land should be saved from development and returned to Māori ownership 2
  • The site held irreplaceable archaeological and cultural significance 1
  • In 2017, SOUL even appealed to the United Nations, which recommended the government review the SHA designation to assess its conformity with the Treaty of Waitangi 4

It's worth noting that the situation was not without internal complexity — some mana whenua (people with ancestral authority over the land) actually supported the housing development going ahead. 2

The 2019 Escalation

Protest numbers swelled significantly in 2019 after eviction notices were served on occupiers. Tensions rose sharply, with clashes between protesters and police, five arrests, and solidarity protests in other cities across the country. 14 King Tūheitia Paki, the leader of the Kīngitanga (Māori King) movement, visited the site and offered to facilitate talks between the parties. 12

The Resolution — and What Came Next

In December 2020, the then Labour government purchased the land from Fletcher Building for $29.9 million, with the money coming from the government's land for housing programme. A Memorandum of Understanding between the Crown, Kīngitanga, and Auckland Council was established to guide the way forward. 245

A steering committee — te rōpu whakahaere (the steering group) — was set up by the Kīngitanga to determine the land's future and develop a plan, with five years to do so. 4 However, progress has been slow and contested. As of 2024, the committee had yet to reach a substantive decision, with differing views among the mana whenua groups involved and political pressure from the incoming coalition government to see results. 6

The future of Ihumātao remains unresolved — a continuing reminder of the long shadows cast by colonial land confiscation, and the ongoing challenge of returning mana (authority and prestige) to those who lost it.
