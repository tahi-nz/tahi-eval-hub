Hauora Māori — Health Outcomes for Māori in Aotearoa

The health gap between Māori and non-Māori in New Zealand is one of the country's most persistent and well-documented inequities. While there have been meaningful improvements over time, Māori continue to experience poorer health outcomes across almost every measure compared to non-Māori.

Life Expectancy

The most striking headline statistic is the gap in life expectancy. According to the most recent Stats NZ data (2022–2024), Māori life expectancy at birth is 75.8 years — the lowest of any ethnic group in New Zealand 1. By comparison, people who identify as Pākehā or other ethnicities have a life expectancy of 82.8 years, a gap of roughly seven years 12.

There is a positive note here, however: Māori life expectancy has increased by 3.1 years from the 2005–2007 period to 2022–2024, a larger increase than any other ethnic group over the same timeframe 1. Earlier data from 2012–2014 recorded life expectancy at birth as 77 years for Māori women and 73 years for Māori men, compared to 84 years for non-Māori women and 80 years for non-Māori men 34.

On average, Māori die younger than non-Māori — a pattern consistently recorded in national data 5.

Chronic Disease

The disparities extend across a wide range of serious conditions:

Heart disease is one of the starkest examples. A 2025 report by Otago University, commissioned by the Kia Manawanui Trust, found that Māori and Pasifika (Pacific peoples) die from heart disease on average ten years younger than Pākehā 67. Māori also have the highest rate of death from heart disease of any ethnic group in New Zealand 8. Data from the 2010s showed Māori were dying from heart disease at more than twice the rate of non-Māori 9.

Cancer figures are similarly concerning. In the 2010s, Māori men were almost three times as likely as non-Māori men to die of lung cancer; Māori women were over four times as likely as non-Māori women 9.

Diabetes has reached near-epidemic levels in some Māori communities. Māori were twice as likely as non-Māori to have diabetes, with higher complication rates as well 9. In the 2000s, it was estimated that well over half of those with diabetes in some Māori communities didn't even know they had it 10.

Child and Youth Health

Māori and Pasifika children are disproportionately affected by respiratory conditions, rheumatic fever and heart disease, skin infections, dental disease, and mental health problems 11. Hospitalisation rates for respiratory conditions among Māori and Pasifika children have been largely attributed to disparities in access to warm, dry housing — a direct consequence of income inequality 12.

While there have been some improvements — meningococcal admissions and skin infection rates among young Māori declined between 2010 and 2014 — the rates of skin infections among Māori aged 0–24 remained twice as high as those for non-Māori youth even after that decline 12.

Mental Health

The Mental Health and Wellbeing Commission's latest reporting (June 2026) paints a troubling picture. Some 22.5 percent of Māori experienced "high" or "very high" psychological distress in the four weeks prior to being surveyed — well above the 14 percent national average 13. The age-standardised rate of suicide deaths is highest among Māori men aged 25 to 44 years 1415.

Despite this elevated need, Māori are not being adequately prioritised for mental health support. Fewer people can access kaupapa Māori (Māori-centred) primary mental health services, funding for those services has not kept pace with broader mental health investment, and only 29 percent of Māori accessing specialist services use kaupapa Māori services 1415.

In inpatient settings, Māori in adult services were 6.6 times more likely to be placed in seclusion (solitary confinement) than non-Māori — a practice the commission describes as often traumatising 15.

Access to Care

Access to healthcare remains a significant barrier. Factors including cost, availability of doctors, geographic isolation, and the attitudes of medical personnel all contribute to poorer health outcomes for Māori 10. Māori living in rural areas face compounding disadvantages — Māori under 30 living remotely were twice as likely to die from a preventable disease as their urban peers 16.

Research also shows that Māori with mental health conditions were 7.7 percent more likely than non-Māori to report difficulties securing a GP appointment, with experiences of feeling less heard and respected by healthcare providers 17.

A Long-Standing Inequity

These disparities are not new. Health differences between Māori and Pākehā have been documented since the 19th century, and despite significant improvements — particularly in the decades following World War II — the gap has proven stubbornly persistent 1819. As researchers note, despite great improvements and a significant rise in life expectancy, Māori are still worse affected than non-Māori by almost every known health condition 9.

The causes are understood to be deeply structural — rooted in poverty, housing quality, access to healthcare, and the legacy of historical inequities 1011.
