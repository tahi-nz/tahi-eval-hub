The Resource Management Act: Aotearoa's Planning Backbone

The Resource Management Act (RMA) 1991 is New Zealand's principal piece of environmental and planning legislation. Hailed at the time as the first comprehensive law in the world to require sustainable management of resources, it replaced roughly 80 earlier acts and orders in one sweeping reform 1. Its architect was Sir Geoffrey Palmer, who described it as 'one of the most comprehensive reforms in the Western world' 2.

At its core, the RMA requires all developments — with the exception of mineral extraction, which is covered separately by the Crown Minerals Act 1991 — to avoid, mitigate, or remedy adverse environmental effects 1. Resource consents for the use of water, air, soil, and land must be obtained from the relevant regional, city, or district councils, with provision for public participation in decision-making 1.

The act also replaced the old Town and Country Planning Act and required councils to prepare new district plans to manage land use in an environmentally sustainable way. An Environment Court replaced the Planning Tribunal 3. Its philosophical ethos, at least initially, was 'more free market than regulatory' — as long as there was no environmental harm, people were largely free to build as they wished 3.

What the RMA Covers

The RMA's reach is broad. It controls land use, manages water quality, regulates air emissions, and protects coastal areas and estuaries 4567. Regional councils are responsible for monitoring these areas and issuing permits and consents accordingly 85.

Notably, the 1991 act was a landmark moment for Māori. Prior legislation had largely ignored Māori historical, spiritual, and cultural concerns. The RMA explicitly recognised Māori spiritual and cultural values and the principles of the Treaty of Waitangi. Environmental developments required consultation with iwi (tribes), and resource consent applications had to be sent to iwi authorities 9.

However, not everything falls under the RMA's umbrella. Biosecurity — including border protection for agriculture and forestry — is governed separately under the Biosecurity Act 1993 10.

The Case for Reform

Despite its ambitions, the RMA has been criticised from multiple directions over the years. Palmer himself acknowledged it wasn't perfect: 'You can have development, or you can have sustainability. There's an inherent contradiction in this which we haven't resolved yet' 11.

Environmental advocates have argued that successive governments failed to provide adequate policy guidance to regional councils, and that councils themselves had increasingly become 'development agencies on behalf of government', undermining the act's protective intent 12. Parliamentary Commissioner for the Environment Jan Wright warned that proposed changes amounted to a 'Trojan horse', arguing the RMA itself provides the balance to economic imperatives and 'is not, and should not become, an economic development act' 13.

On the other side, critics have long complained the RMA makes it too hard and too costly to build housing, infrastructure, and renewable energy. Federated Farmers called the current system 'broken', with farmers spending excessive time and money on resource consents for straightforward activities 14.

There has been bipartisan recognition that the act has both failed to protect the environment sufficiently and wrapped projects in excessive red tape 15.

The Coalition Government's Reform Programme

The current National-led coalition government has pursued the most ambitious overhaul of the RMA in its history, structured in three phases.

Phase one involved repealing legislation introduced by the previous Labour government and making urgent early amendments, including revisions to stock exclusion rules, winter grazing, Te Mana o te Wai (the concept of water as a taonga — a treasured resource), mining consenting, and the suspension of Significant Natural Area (SNA) requirements 16.

Phase two introduced a second RMA Amendment Bill focused on infrastructure, energy, housing, farming, and natural hazards, alongside the single largest package of national direction changes in New Zealand's history — seven new national direction instruments and amendments to 14 existing ones 17. A Fast-track Approvals Bill was also introduced as a 'one-stop shop' consenting regime for significant projects 18.

Phase three — the most sweeping — proposes to scrap the RMA entirely and replace it with two new laws 1915:

  • A Natural Environment Act, focused on managing environmental effects from activities, including setting quantified environmental limits at a regional level 15
  • A Planning Act, focused on enabling urban development and infrastructure 19

RMA Reform minister Chris Bishop said the goal is to cut administrative and compliance costs by 45 percent, grounded in the principle of 'the enjoyment of property rights' — allowing people to do more on their own land as long as it doesn't harm others 20. The reforms also propose more standardised zoning nationally (New Zealand currently has 1,175 different zone types across its councils, compared to just 13 in Japan) and a new planning disputes tribunal for faster, lower-cost resolution of disagreements 2015.

Notably, the reforms would remove the Treaty of Waitangi clause currently in the RMA 20.

In December 2025, the coalition formally introduced the Planning Bill and Natural Environment Bill to Parliament, with the aim of passing them before the 2026 election 21. Labour leader Chris Hipkins indicated his party would not repeal the legislation if elected, though it would likely make amendments — suggesting the 'repeal and replace cycle' that has plagued RMA reform for decades may finally be drawing to a close 21.

Reactions

Responses to the reforms have been mixed. Federated Farmers welcomed the shift, saying the RMA had been 'the single biggest handbrake on growing agricultural productivity' 21. Local Government New Zealand expressed readiness to adapt, though its representatives acknowledged a 'tsunami of change' ahead for councils 21. Environmental groups such as Forest & Bird have raised concerns about the pace of reform and potential erosion of environmental protections, particularly around freshwater 2215.
