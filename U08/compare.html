The 1981 Springbok Tour: New Zealand's Closest Thing to Civil War

Few events in New Zealand history have cut as deep as the 1981 Springbok rugby tour — 56 days of conflict that pitted neighbour against neighbour, family against family, and forced the country to confront who it really was.

Background: A Long and Troubled History

Rugby was the national game of both New Zealand and white South Africa, and the two countries had been competing fiercely since the 1920s. But South Africa's apartheid system — a formal policy of racial segregation established in 1948 that excluded Black and 'coloured' players from national sports teams — had long shadowed the relationship. 1

The tensions were not new. As far back as 1937, the Springboks had refused to play an all-Māori team. In 1960, 160,000 New Zealanders signed a petition opposing an All Blacks tour of South Africa. A Labour government cancelled a planned Springbok tour in 1973. But in 1975, the National Party made freedom of 'sporting contacts' an election issue and won a landslide victory. 2

When the Springbok team arrived on 19 July 1981 — the first tour since 1965 — the stage was already set for a reckoning. 3

Two Sides of a Nation

At its core, the debate was about New Zealand's national identity. As one source put it, for some, rugby was the heart of New Zealand's identity — a country of strong outdoor men whose contests with South Africa were central to their world view. For others, New Zealand's identity was as an opponent of racism and a fighter for human rights, and sport had to reflect those values. 4

The division wasn't just ideological — it was geographical. Support for the tour was strongest in rural areas, while opposition was concentrated in cities. Long-standing prejudices resurfaced, with pro-tour supporters and anti-tour activists hurling insults across the divide. 5

Prime Minister Robert Muldoon, despite New Zealand being a party to the Commonwealth's Gleneagles Agreement against sporting contact with apartheid South Africa, refused to stop the tour. Many New Zealanders felt betrayed — not by the New Zealand Rugby Football Union, whose position had always been clear, but by a government that appeared to step back from a fundamental issue of integrity. 67

The Protests: Unprecedented in New Zealand History

The opposition was massive and organised. In Auckland, the Mobilisation to Stop the Tour (MOST) was established; in Wellington, the Coalition to Oppose the Springbok Tour (COST). The umbrella group Halt All Racist Tours (HART) coordinated much of the national effort. 89

An estimated 65,000 people took to the streets in the first major mobilisation, and around 150,000 people turned out across the three-month tour — the biggest mass protest New Zealand had ever seen. 610

Māori activists played a significant role. The Patu Squad in Auckland, led by Ripeka Evans, Donna Awatere and Hone Harawira, had a core of around 100 members, mostly Māori, and represented a distinct strand of protest that connected opposition to apartheid with the fight against racism at home. 11

The churches were also involved. St Matthew's Anglican Church in Auckland hosted anti-tour meetings, and Archbishop Paul Reeves was prominent in opposing the tour. 12

Violence and Chaos

Anti-tour campaigners blocked roads and invaded rugby grounds. Running street battles between protesters and supporters became commonplace, and police batoned protesters — with bloody results. 3 In Wellington, protesters who pushed into a police line on Molesworth Street were heavily batoned. 13

The Hamilton test had to be cancelled after protesters occupied the field at Rugby Park, with red smoke from flares drifting across the ground as 18,000 fans looked on. A stolen plane — piloted by ex-fighter pilot Pat McQuarrie — circled overhead, and police commissioner Bob Walton called the game off in the interests of public safety. 6

The tour's most iconic moment came in the final test at Eden Park in Auckland, when a light plane flew over the ground dropping flour bombs, pamphlets and stink bombs onto the pitch, striking All Black prop Gary Knight. The match continued, with New Zealand winning 25–22 in injury time. 14

By the tour's end, more than 1,500 people had been charged with protest-related offences, and the tour had cost taxpayers $7.2 million (equivalent to $27.5 million in 2013 terms). 28

The Legacy

The 1981 tour galvanised and strengthened New Zealand's anti-racist movement. After 1981, no more racially selected Springbok teams toured New Zealand, and in 1984 the Labour government banned South African sports teams from the country entirely. 82

The tour also sparked a broader national conversation. Attention turned to domestic race relations and to the need for New Zealanders to better understand Te Tiriti o Waitangi (the Treaty of Waitangi). 15 Some New Zealanders with a conscience shifted their sporting allegiances, following the All Whites to the 1982 football World Cup, and New Zealand women began to challenge the dominance of male sports in the media. 4

In a remarkable full circle, South Africa's two votes are believed to have been critical in New Zealand winning the right to host the 2011 Rugby World Cup — with South African officials publicly thanking New Zealand protest leaders like John Minto and Trevor Richards for their role in helping end apartheid. 16
